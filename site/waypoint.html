The Waypoint loop

Four steps, repeated. Nothing enters the backlog that you have not chosen at step two.

Most Salesforce orgs peak on the day they go live.

The project ends, the consultants leave, and the platform starts drifting. Fields get added without a reason. Reports multiply. Adoption slips quietly. Two years later somebody proposes a rebuild, and the business pays a second time for capability it already owned.

Nobody owns the roadmap

After go-live the backlog becomes whatever was escalated most recently. Improvement stops being planned and starts being reactive.

Change without a record

Fields, flows and permissions accumulate. Nobody can say why any of it is there, which makes the next change slower than the last one.

Adoption decays quietly

Usage falls well before anyone reports a problem. By the time it shows up in a number, the habit has already gone.

Paid-for capability sits unused

Three Salesforce releases a year ship features you are already licensed for. Without someone watching, they stay switched off.

One loop, run every quarter

The same four steps each quarter, so the platform improves on a cadence rather than whenever something breaks loudly enough.

01

Review

We look at what the platform is actually doing: adoption by team, data quality, automation failing quietly, technical debt, and the capability in the last three releases that you are paying for and not using.

02

Prioritise

Findings come to you with an effort estimate against each one, and you decide what is worth doing. Nothing enters the backlog that you have not chosen, and anything you decline comes off the list rather than reappearing.

03

Build

The agreed work is built, tested and released by the same senior practitioners who reviewed it. Every change is documented as it lands, so the record is never reconstructed from memory six months later.

04

Measure

The next review opens with what changed and what it moved. Where something did not move, we say so plainly and it comes off the list rather than being restated as a win.

What a Waypoint month actually contains

A named senior practitioner

The same person every month. They learn your org properly, and you stop re-explaining it at the start of every request.

Agreed build capacity

A set allocation of build days each month, spent on the backlog you prioritised. Unused days roll forward one month so a quiet period is not simply lost.

Quarterly platform review

Adoption, data quality, automation health, security and technical debt, written up with findings ranked by what they are costing you rather than by how interesting they are.

Release readiness

Three Salesforce releases a year, checked against your org before they land rather than after something stops working on a Monday morning.

Documentation that stays current

Configuration and change documentation updated as work lands, plus a decision register with the reasoning kept alongside each decision.

Advisory access

The conversation about whether something is worth doing, before it becomes a request. Often the most valuable part, and the hardest to buy by the hour.

How it is priced

A monthly fee set by the build allocation you want, agreed before you start. Unused days roll forward one month. Change the allocation, pause, or stop, with a month's notice.

What it does not cover

Work larger than the monthly allocation is quoted as a separate fixed-price piece rather than absorbed quietly. Salesforce licence costs, third-party product support and end-user training beyond ad hoc guidance sit outside it.

Why there is no term

Enable does not use lock-in contracts, non-competes or minimum terms, and Waypoint is no exception. A programme that has to be contractually enforced is not delivering. If it is worth having, the invoice will not be the reason you stay.
