The work / Hato Hone St John

Building teams that ship faster, with AI in the loop.

Hato Hone St John wanted delivery to move at a different pace. Enable worked with their teams to stand up rapid AI-assisted development practice: not a tool rollout, but a way of working that gets from idea to something running in a fraction of the time.

The organisation

Hato Hone St John has been operating in New Zealand since 1885. It is a charity, it employs and enrols many thousands of paid staff and volunteers, and it provides the overwhelming majority of the country’s emergency and non-emergency ambulance coverage, alongside first aid training, event medical cover, community health programmes and support lines for people living alone.

That is a wide operational surface, and it is one where software delays have a cost that is not measured in revenue. Technology that takes eighteen months to land is technology that did not help this year.

The situation

The teams were capable and the backlog was longer than the delivery capacity, which is the ordinary condition of almost every in-house technology function. The question was not whether people were working hard. It was whether the way work moved from idea to running software could be changed at all, rather than simply resourced harder.

AI tooling was the obvious lever and also the obvious trap. Handing a team a licence and calling it transformation is how most organisations spend a year discovering nothing changed.

What we did

01

Started with how work actually moves

Before any tooling, we followed real work through the current process: where it waited, where it was rewritten, where a decision took three weeks because the right four people were never in the same conversation. Speed problems are usually queueing problems wearing a technical costume.

02

Stood up rapid development teams

Small teams set up to go from a stated problem to something running, with AI assistance built into the loop rather than bolted on at the coding step. Scoping, drafting, reviewing and testing all change when the cost of a first version falls.

03

Built the judgement, not just the habit

The hard part of AI-assisted delivery is knowing when to trust the output and when not to. We worked on review practice, on what gets checked and by whom, and on where a human has to stay in the loop regardless of how convincing the draft looks.

04

Left the capability with their teams

The measure of this work is that it keeps going without us. Practice documented, patterns written down, and the people who will run it afterwards involved from the first week rather than briefed at the end.

What changed

From idea to running, faster

The gap between someone describing a need and someone using the result closed materially. That shift compounds: shorter cycles mean more attempts, and more attempts mean better answers.

Fewer things waiting

A lot of the gain was not in writing code faster. It was in the queue before the code, where work sat waiting on a decision, a spec or a review.

The teams own it

The practice sits with Hato Hone St John, not with a partner they have to re-engage to keep it running. That was the point of the engagement rather than a nice side effect.

What we would tell you

Rapid AI development is a ways-of-working change that happens to involve tools. Organisations that buy the tools and skip the working practice get a faster first draft and the same delivery date, because the bottleneck was never typing.

Review discipline matters more, not less. When output is cheap and plausible, the scarce thing is the judgement to reject it. Build that explicitly or it will not happen.

And in an organisation where the work is clinical and operational rather than commercial, “move fast” needs a clear boundary around where it does not apply. Agreeing that boundary early is what makes the speed acceptable everywhere else.

Delivery not moving at the pace you need?

If your teams are capable and the backlog still grows, the constraint is usually in how work moves rather than how hard people work. That is a conversation worth having.
